### 3 Ways to Influence Your AI Visibility
There are actually not that many ways you can influence your visibility within an LLM. One is increasing your visibility within training data. These models are trained on vast corpuses of web data — they scrape the web, Google Books, the Common Crawl dataset, and update periodically (every ~6 months). If you can make your brand appear more often in that training data, theoretically you can be recommended more often. The limitation: there's not that much you can actually do to influence this, and it's probably not the most actionable thing.

The second point is much more important: increasing your visibility in RAG data. LLMs use retrieval augmented generation — they go out and search the web (sometimes Google, sometimes Bing) when training data doesn't suffice. That's good news for us because we know how to appear within search. That is SEO. That is content marketing.

The last one, briefly, is black hat. There are temporary ways you can boost visibility in a particular conversation or model. I don't think that's worth any legitimate brand considering long-term because it doesn't last and causes more trouble than it's worth.

One thing to add: increasing visibility in training data and in citations/sources is essentially the same tactics. You need to see where your competitors are mentioned, where you are mentioned, where your industry is mentioned, and get mentions there. If a chatbot does a search and finds those pages, great; and if a provider retrains on a more recent snapshot, they use essentially the same pages. So you're killing two birds with one stone. (There's also "AI model collapse" concern — AI trained on AI content over cycles generates bad results — so providers are wary of retraining.)

### The "Era of Off-Page SEO": Why Mentions are the New Backlinks
That segues to probably the most useful discrete tactic: building off-site mentions. A big part of how LLMs understand your brand is based on where you appear in training data and on the web — what topics you're mentioned alongside, what other brands. Patrick Stox refers to this as the era of off-page SEO. The content about you on other pages is in some ways more valuable than the content on your own site. And these don't even have to be links. There's still tons of value in a backlink, but now there's a lot of value to just being mentioned without the link, because the text itself is useful for training these models.

We tested this. We looked at the factors that correlate with the number of times a brand appears in AI Overviews. By far the strongest correlation — almost 0.67 — was branded web mentions. If your brand is mentioned in a ton of places on the web, that correlates very highly with appearing in lots of AI conversations.

One of the best ways: look for the domains in your industry most commonly cited by AI models. They have preferences for where they prioritize getting information. User-generated content is huge — Reddit is hugely visible in AI search, similar with Quora. Third-party review sites too — G2 has suffered from diminishing organic traffic but their visibility in AI search is huge. YouTube transcripts/videos get recommended very often. We have a tool (Brand Radar) that helps you find the most commonly cited domains. Put in a topic (e.g. "wild camping") and you see the top domains and individual pages most commonly mentioned. If you can get your brand on those pages, your AI visibility shoots up.

[Tim:] Citations are the new backlinks in the AI search era — you want to know in your industry the most cited websites and pages. My common advice to anyone saying "SEO takes time for new websites": go to the websites that already rank for the keywords and topics you want, and figure out how to get mentioned there — partnership, affiliate deal, advertisement. You get "secondhand search traffic." The same principle applies to AI search.

### Optimizing the Content Types AI Prefers to Cite
AI systems prefer to send traffic to certain types of pages. The theory: if those pages get surfaced a lot, spend more time and energy on them. Caveat: attributable traffic from AI assistants isn't the whole picture — most of a buying journey can happen without ever clicking a link.

We looked at the n-grams (fragments in the URL) of pages most referred to from AI search. Common themes: informational content (blogs, guides) cited heavily; comparison content (best, top, versus) surprisingly heavily cited; and core site pages (homepage, about) used a lot as AI tries to back up what it says. We compared a sample of AI-search traffic vs traditional-search traffic: LLMs were much more likely to send traffic to core website pages, to documents (dusty PDFs that get no search traffic), to research content (data they can reference), and to video content (proportionally about twice as many visits went to videos from LLMs than from traditional search). The inverse is also true: interactive tools, listings, collections, e-commerce, navigation pages — these get tons of traditional search traffic but very little from LLMs.

### How to Write for AI (and Humans)
A few things seem to have benefit. Probably the strongest factor is content freshness. We looked at ~17 million citations to see the average age of a URL. ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini vastly prefer fresher, newer pages than what's commonly referenced in organic search. I don't think you should update every page every day — this is probably more reflective of the types of queries that trigger RAG. If a query can be answered by the model's own knowledge it does that; if it's a new topic it runs a search and finds new content.

And simple writing. People talk about chunking and get very technical. Easier: write in simple, straightforward ways that are easy for both people and AI to understand. BLUF — bottom line up front: open with the clearest, most succinct articulation of your idea. Use declarative sentences — confident opinions, definitive terms. Few subject-predicate hops — don't write long meandering sentences. Entity richness — at Ahrefs Evolve last year, Bernard Huang from Clearscope gave a talk showing AI outputs prefer entity-rich text; he found a strong correlation between mentioning more topics/brands/discrete things and the likelihood of being cited. Global document context — on a very long page, at regular intervals remind the reader and AI what the page is about, in case it gets chopped into pieces. A lot of it boils down to simple, clear writing with a very clear purpose.

### Finding and Fixing "Hallucinated URLs" from AI
LLMs hallucinate — they can make URLs up. When we looked at our website, almost 4% of all visits from LLMs went to made-up URLs (e.g. ahrefs.com/keywords, which sounds plausible but doesn't exist). In Ahrefs Web Analytics (free up to a million page views) we now have a dedicated filter for finding hallucinated URLs — pages that get visits from LLM traffic but return a 404.

### The Untapped Training Data Sources LLMs Love
Some novel training-data sources traditionally don't impact organic search but may be worth optimizing. Best example: GitHub — every GitHub repo your company has is almost guaranteed to be trained on. Make sure repos have lots of context (explain what they're about, reference your brand/products, link to helpful content). Wikipedia pages are guaranteed to be trained upon. Also original research papers, patents, even books — things that get read by LLMs and can impact brand visibility.

### How to Perform a Content Gap Analysis for the AI Era
Entity gaps — like content gap analysis but for the AI era. AI tries to mention your brand based on its understanding via co-mentions — the words used near your brand. E.g. Patagonia is mentioned alongside "Nano Puff hoodie," which is mentioned alongside "wild camping," "backpacking," "lightweight." But there can be a disconnect between the topics you'd like to be visible for and the topics you actually are. Use Brand Radar: pick your brand, compare to your biggest competitor, see conversations where competitors are mentioned and you are not. Example: Fjällräven vs Patagonia — "school backpacks" is something Fjällräven dominates and Patagonia is absent from. If Patagonia wanted that visibility, they could create content on-site and off-site to help AI develop that understanding. [Tim: Brand Radar is unique because it's based on millions of existing prompts rather than you supplying a few hundred prompts — so you avoid selection bias.]

### The #1 Technical Mistake in Answer Engine Optimization
Avoid too much JavaScript. Most AI crawlers still do not render JavaScript. Modern Google search is pretty good at JavaScript-heavy sites, but AI search is not as mature. If you have important pages with important info/products that rely heavily on JavaScript, there's a good chance they're basically invisible within AI search. This will probably change — AI crawlers lag behind but are catching up quickly (around May, Cloudflare data showed ~25% of all bot requests came from AI bots, growing).

### Fan-Out Queries: How AI Actually Searches Google
When an AI assistant runs a search, what is it searching for? My first thought: is it entering the user prompt verbatim into Google? No. We found only 12% of the links cited by ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot were actually in the top 10 results for the user prompt entered into the AI interface. Instead it's "fan-out queries" — the AI thinks about the prompt and asks what simpler Google searches it can run to retrieve the info, combine it, and answer. The average query is ~25 words, which isn't always good for Googling, so it generates query fan-outs. There was a window where you could see the fan-out queries in ChatGPT web search via the JSON response: "what are the best cheap sunglasses of 2025" became things like "men affordable sunglasses review 2025," "best cheap men's sunglasses 2025." Perplexity still lets you see these. (This is possibly why we saw inflated impressions in Google Search Console — one AI query triggers 3-5 searches; some tools do 10-25 fan-outs at a time.)

I wouldn't create a page for every fan-out query — without clickstream data we don't know how often individual queries are searched. It's more useful for understanding how fan-outs are constructed. The main actionable takeaway: there's more room for different opinions. Previously, to optimize a page for Google you made it look similar to the top-ranking pages — the industry trended toward similarity. Now, because there's so much variance in the fan-out queries, there's a chance for your page to show up even when it doesn't follow the conventional structure. You get a chance to get visits by being different. You no longer need to look at the top-ranking pages necessarily — create your content your way.

### The Spam Strategy You Must Avoid
My last point is about avoiding spam. Some companies publish 20,000-word markdown-formatted AI-generated pages with no links, no images — purely designed for LLM ingestion, not for people. I am seeing these get cited. But it's shortsighted: Google is much less susceptible to that type of gaming (years of practice filtering spam) and is still by far the most dominant traffic source and heavily relied on for RAG. Other AI assistants will become more sophisticated. And most importantly: is it even valuable being cited if someone clicks and sees spammy markdown content nobody cares about? Are they going to buy from you? Avoid the idea of "let's just pump out a bunch of AI content and see what happens."

### A Simple 2-Category Framework for AEO
There are generally two categories all LLM-optimization tactics fall into. One: tactics useful in AI search that are already useful in traditional search (on-site content creation, getting mentioned in third-party content, engaging on Reddit). Anything here is guaranteed useful because it's already useful for SEO. Two: novel/exotic things that may be useful in AI search but have no usefulness in traditional search (llms.txt, chunking in very technical ways). A lot of people index on these because they're trendy and get attention. If you're trying to grow brand visibility, do stuff in that first category that also has utility in traditional search — there's tons of overlap.

### AI Search vs. Google's AI Overviews
[Tim:] AI search (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini) is not killing traditional Google search. People seeing traffic drops — it's not because people switched to ChatGPT, it's because Google is displaying AI Overviews that eat traffic. (In Ahrefs there's a filter for keywords that display AI Overviews.) Rand Fishkin did a study with clickstream data: people are still using Google as their default; ChatGPT adoption is growing (went from ~8% to ~22%) but it's additive — people aren't migrating, they switch between the two. Do not put all your efforts into AI without caring about search. Search still works.
