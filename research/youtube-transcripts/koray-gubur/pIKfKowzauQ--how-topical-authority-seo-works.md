### Topical authority explained to a "5-year-old" (the cookies example)
Imagine ranking cookies. You cluster/classify cookies by taste, brand, and features, then rank them by eating order — which cookie would you eat first? That cookie represents your page/article; the kid is the search engine. But it's about the queries: if users don't search "chocolate," "dip," or "chip," that word has little weight. You might merge words ("chocolate cookies") and put "dips" as a bottom heading if there's no query for it. If you open a new page for every entity/type, you bloat your website. The goal: fewer pages with more value, based on queries. From the search engine's point of view, what matters is answering a query — if there's no query, it doesn't matter much.

"Cheese" can act like a modifier: depending on the most important word in the query and user preferences, entities switch with each other. So creating a topical map is not making a list of concepts/phrases/keywords — you need to understand how the search engine would present these and how they connect.

### Building a topical map for "chocolate cookies"
Insert verbs between "chocolate" and "cookie" (cooked, sweet) and add words to the end (recipe, brands, manufacturers, effects/side effects, "is it healthy"). Every combination gets matched to queries on a wall, ordered by likely search intent. If a user wants to cook it, "recipe" features are heavier; if they want to buy, the local map pack appears. Probabilities change by user cluster, even morning vs night. This forces you to add every context. If you're a recipe website, catch the search intent at the top (restaurants/markets/manufacturers if that's what the query is about), then in the middle connect the page to yourself (your brand/recipes). This way you satisfy users interested in you plus users who aren't — and you create a duplication of the SERP in a proper order.

This is macro vs micro context, main content vs supplementary content. According to what you do and how you make money, create supporting pages: a gluten-free website → "gluten-free cookies"; a celiac-disease website → "gluten-free cookie recipes" + "celiac disease symptoms" as internal links; a parenthood website → "cookies for kids." Catch the query with the context, create enough content to rank, then connect it to yourself from other areas.

Most people only check the three top-ranking pages per query, but those top-3 often don't align with each other. For our topic, we get all the ranking (~600 domains), analyze them, and understand how Google classifies them — including which appear in Google Discover (which signals more relevance). To be a topical authority you need to push another website out of that state, one competitor at a time.

### Google Discover
Google Discover is a "query-less" search — no one searches; Google suggests. It orders topics, orders websites, matches topic to website. Entertaining/interesting entities rank better. In one case study they got Discover traffic in five months from zero. Discover-traffic pages were about politicians, investors, persons, books, podcasts — "fun" entities. Boring/scientific topics (botanics) don't appear much. Discover content should be interesting, entertaining, intriguing.

### The knowledge-hub / EEAT shift
[Host:] Many affiliate sites were hit by the helpful-content update because they're essentially "doorway pages" (click the affiliate link and leave). Now your site almost needs to be a knowledge hub. Instead of "best running shoes for men / women / marathon / treadmill," talk about best places to run, vitamins before a marathon, how to run faster, foods to run faster.

Koray's three points: (1) If you're not a brand — no one searches for you, no direct traffic, no prominence — Google is less sensitive about demoting you; you might not even be a real business. (2) Most affiliate sites put the highest-commission option at the top, not the best — an unhidden sponsorship, not that different from link schemes. (3) Most don't give information beyond common sense; even "how to run faster" lacks unique info, proof, or experience. You need real expertise, perspective, and experience. You should also define/explain everything appearing in the phrases (not the history of shoes, but e.g. the places to use specific shoes, the materials). Example: a bookstore he published became the fifth-biggest e-commerce site in Turkey — on product (sales) pages they gave the cover materials, the meaning of the cover, refund/shipment policy, alternatives — explaining the product from every angle. They connected every book to an author, every author to a biography, to genres, to other novels and characters.

When you rank for a certain entity group from a topic, the engine picks up your expertise and prioritizes you — because they can trust you and it's cheaper (they can't check 5 million websites per query). Links make it faster/more permanent, but if the engine finds a good site about a topic even without links, it will still rank it (ranking it means satisfying users at lower cost; they won't wait two years for natural links). After quality tests and a broad-core update, it explodes. But affiliate marketers who win a core update often get greedy, publish irrelevant content, dilute topicality, decrease overall quality, and then lose the next core update.

### Source context
[Host:] After a positive ranking state, if you expand to e.g. Thailand keywords, the source context of your site needs to be similar to a Booking.com/TripAdvisor for that to rank. Koray: the search engine has to find a usability in your site. Global sites like TripAdvisor get all the tours, hotels, roads, attractions, health policies for every country, have thousands of referring domains, are real-world brands with millions of reviews (and a forum — Google promotes forums). Local sites struggle against them. "Trending node": create pages to exploit trends — during the Ukraine war one site ranked higher because it had many Ukraine articles; popularity/traffic from somewhere lifts other pages too. After the helpful-content and core updates, mostly forums and major global companies rank, because others "just pump up content."

### The "Wikipedia game" / knowledge hub
[Host:] My theory now: be the knowledge hub for your industry. If you talk about cookies, also cover "can pregnant women eat cookies," "what temperature to bake cookies." Koray: after Bill Slawski passed away, Koray felt all that knowledge would be forgotten because no one reads, so he wanted to take it further in a practical, simpler way. iGaming is paying attention now, which signals things are changing. On a Bill Slawski discussion years ago, someone asked "I have a movie website, do I have to win an Oscar?" — you don't have to win the Oscar to rank, but at least be sincere about what you do. Most sites are doorway pages. Google patents call it "composite search score" — instead of individually scoring each site, they create a composition from sites and score each composition; if aggregators make more sense, aggregators rank higher. If your site loses rankings, it doesn't mean you did something wrong — Google may just prefer a different type of website in that situation.

[Host:] After a core update, look at what's ranking now and make sure the intent/format is similar to what you're doing (e.g., switch a numbered list to an HTML table; application queries need a form). Koray: what you do determines who you are (source context) — you can't rank for "credit application" without an application form; if you're not the application source, there's no reason to rank because users have to go elsewhere to apply (you're just a bridge).

### Quick-fire definitions
- **Semantic triple:** subject, predicate, object — remove unnecessary words to get the essence ("Tom Hanks acted in Green Mile").
- **Source context:** your connection with the query — as service provider, aggregator, affiliate, bank, car-rental server, etc. Who you are determines whether you rank. Google's helpful-content guidance says a site should have a primary focus.
- **Query expansion/augmentation:** the query written into the search bar isn't the same as the engine's reference; "best cookie" may become "buy best cookie." You should see the non-written words; if you can calculate those non-written versions like the engine does, you rank higher.
