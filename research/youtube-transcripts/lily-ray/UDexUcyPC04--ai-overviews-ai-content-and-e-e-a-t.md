**Intro / who she is.** Lily Ray's current title is VP of SEO Strategy & Research at the agency Amsive (7+ years). She's done SEO ~15 years, 12-13 of which in agency environments, across hundreds of clients — grew up in e-commerce SEO, now more focused on news and publisher clients. Most people know her for EEAT and for analysis of Google core updates (why some sites drop and some rise). She got into update analysis ~2015-2018 when a lot of clients were impacted by algorithm updates.

**AI Overviews — what to expect.** It's really hard to fully understand the impact because there's no way to track it perfectly. Tools like Semrush, Ahrefs, and especially ZipTie give visibility into AI Overview rankings — you can put keywords in and see who's being cited and what the AI Overview response says. You need to think beyond just organic rankings and track AI Overviews. Even within Search Console it's tricky — a lot of AI Overview citations are reported as position number one.

Does impact depend on industry/type of site? It depends on everything. You can also see situations with a lot more traffic — sometimes an AI Overview cites the same website five times, then below that you get an organic result from that company, and the company might even be mentioned in the AI Overview (e.g., "what's the best dog food company" lists the company as a recommendation + three citations + an organic link → expect an uptick). But generally, on average, you're going to see less traffic.

**Quality of results.** Much better than when it came out. She's used it since beta and it's been getting better every day. The problem she (and many) have with LLMs: there's always going to be some level of hallucination, and a failure to understand irony or humor — sometimes it presents answers a human would never have written or interpreted that way. There have always been inaccuracies in Google's results, but AI Overviews tend to have more.

**AI Mode** (announced the prior week). If you asked Google, they'd say it's still in testing — we don't know if/how it'll roll out publicly or how much it'll change the look and feel. The way it's presented now, it looks like a new layer on top of Google Search; theoretically Google searchers might start in AI Mode next year. From what she's seen so far it's very similar to AI Overviews. Google is trying to add opportunities for sites to appear, but the question is whether users get what they need from the answer and click on anything.

**Do we need a new approach?** A lot of the SEO fundamentals will be the same, but it's important to pay attention to how LLMs / AI Mode / AI Overviews talk about your core questions. New tools do LLM tracking to understand the answers, who appears, which brands are recommended. It's a new form of SEO — not just about traffic, but about brand visibility and being mentioned in AI Overviews / ChatGPT.

**Missing EEAT in AI engines.** She uses Perplexity and GPT a lot and feels they sometimes don't look at EEAT at all — there's a much more raw list of sites that appear in both ChatGPT and AI Overviews. Good in the sense of more diversity (sites not ranking in the top 100 can appear in an AI Overview); Google has said AI Overviews help more websites get more traffic, implying broader diversity beyond positions 1-10. The downside: all the work Google did for years to build up EEAT — sometimes a website with no authority or a spammy site appears for a health query, which isn't how it's supposed to work. You'll often see sites nobody's heard of, or a weird forum post, cited in the AI answer. It's problematic.

**EEAT as a checklist.** For most people EEAT is a checklist of tactics to influence an invisible score (there is no EEAT score). She's never endorsed quantifying EEAT — it can't be quantified. Checklists aren't bad as a starting point or template, but it's important to think beyond the checklist. You can't just follow the same thing everyone else did — that's not how SEO works anymore. You have to think about how to convey to your specific audience, in your niche, that you can be trusted — and that should differ from person to person.

**B2B experts with a small footprint.** A lot of B2B companies have very good experts who aren't famous in their niche — maybe they've written 10-20 blog posts on the company site. You can create author pages and add structured data (the checklist), but the real thing is that the person should become more of an expert: getting on stage, being a guest on podcasts, being interviewed by newspapers or industry sites. Otherwise you're creating tactical artifacts around something that isn't really EEAT. It's personal branding — it's above and beyond SEO; it's good for business. People like to follow real people, influencers, and experts. When she works with companies on EEAT, a lot of it involves taking experts with a small online footprint — maybe a recording of them speaking at a conference, a podcast — and doing a bit more marketing around what they've actually done, or getting them featured in an academic journal.

That approach (experts/influencers who represent a bigger company) is where marketing has been going. In the SEO space, a person everyone knows kind of represents a company, so you hire that company because of that person's work — and this extends far beyond SEO. Younger generations follow people on Instagram and TikTok; when you're ready to go on a diet, you hire the company your favorite fitness influencer works at because you trust their content.

**Checklist vs. consequence.** If a checklist helps you make sure you're covering everything, or you're stuck and don't know what to work on, it can be helpful. She just discourages any approach that tries to simplify something that shouldn't be simple — you should work with a group of people to come up with unique, original, creative approaches. If everyone does the exact same thing (e.g., everyone hiring the "best freelancers" with "the most expertise"), Google develops ranking demotions and manual actions that say "this is spam, it's not real." Checklists can be used two ways: "this is important for Google so I'm doing it," or "I never thought about how this is valuable for my visitors, so I'm doing it because of that" — the latter is good for EEAT.

**AI content — good or bad?** Not inherently bad, but inherently mediocre — it's repurposing what everyone else has already said. When you ask it to do something creative, funny, or to play a character, it's really impressive; but creating an article from scratch is, by definition, taking what's already out there.

[Host:] Working with B2B companies, it's less about SEO at scale and more about finding the right stories and creating different content forms — start with a podcast/video, create a transcript, then the transcript is easy to turn into an article that's still the person's story. Claude is better than me at transforming the transcript into a good article — so AI improves my content. But AI-generated content in most cases is just a prompt with no input, done at scale.

Lily: important distinction, and I agree 100%. When I said "mediocre," I'm talking about asking it to tell a story or come up with an article about XYZ — that's different from "here's my existing data/article/beliefs, enhance it, summarize it, make it more readable." That's how I use it, and I don't consider that AI content — I consider it AI-assisted processes.

[Host:] Even if Google could detect that a transcript was transformed by Claude, they don't know the original was a transcript — they might think it's AI-generated and punish it. But I'll still create content that way because it's better for my audience.

Lily: I don't think Google would want to punish that. If you have an original transcript that you used AI to repurpose, even if it can pick up that some of the language feels a bit AI (the punctuation AI tools use), the ideas and concepts are original — that's perfectly fine. At the end of the day Google has Gemini and its own generative AI tools they want us to use. It's problematic when you publish something that's completely AI content that's already been written about a thousand times.

[Host:] So how much of the story is new and not available on the internet yet — maybe in combination with other factors (links, no contact details, lots of AI content → a problem; otherwise not). Lily: there are a lot of different signals that work together to show that you're a medium-to-low-quality site mostly focused on SEO and not on solving real user problems.

**Does she use AI?** Every day, all day. Not to generate stuff from scratch (unless she needs inspiration) and not publishing 100% AI content — but throughout the day for summarizing long things, reformatting, creative ideas for articles based on other articles that have performed well. It's a productivity engine that makes her feel like she's supercharging her work.
